A No-Nonsense TOPIK I Study Plan

By Hookitlingo · Free & offline Korean courses

TOPIK I is the entry-level Korean proficiency exam most learners take first, for university applications, visas, or just to have a real benchmark of where they stand. It covers two levels (1 and 2) across listening and reading, no speaking or writing section. Here's the roadmap we use ourselves to structure our free TOPIK prep courses, stripped of filler.

The six-stage path

  • 1
    Hangeul fluencyRead and write without sounding out letter by letter. Non-negotiable. Everything after this assumes reading speed, not just recognition.
  • 2
    Core vocabulary (800-1000 words)High-frequency nouns, verbs, and adjectives that show up constantly across TOPIK I listening and reading passages.
  • 3
    Basic grammar & particles이/가, 은/는, 을/를, 에, 에서, the sentence-building blocks tested directly and required to parse every passage.
  • 4
    Numbers, counters & timeSino-Korean and Native Korean number systems, dates, money, and telling time, a heavily tested, easy-to-underestimate category.
  • 5
    Listening practice with real pacingTOPIK audio moves faster than most textbook audio. Practicing at exam speed matters more than practicing with more material at a slower pace.
  • 6
    Timed practice testsFull-length, under real time pressure. This is where most points get lost, not from not knowing the material, but from running out of time.
The mistake most self-studiers make Jumping straight to practice tests before stage 6 feels productive but backfires. You end up testing gaps you haven't filled yet instead of confirming gaps you have. Follow the order.

How long should this take?

For a consistent 30-45 minutes a day, most learners move through stages 1-5 in about 8-10 weeks, then spend the final 2-3 weeks purely on timed practice tests. If you're starting from zero Korean knowledge, budget closer to 12 weeks total.

What actually moves the score needle

Two things disproportionately affect TOPIK I scores: reading speed (not vocabulary size, you can know a word and still run out of time finding it in a passage) and comfort with numbers/time, which are tested constantly but often under-practiced because they feel "basic."
